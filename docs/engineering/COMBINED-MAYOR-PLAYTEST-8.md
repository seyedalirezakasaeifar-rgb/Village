# Combined mayor economic playtest

Source: integrated 6e8665e3cdee520148f44947e49cdc8e7ddae4c6 plus procurement forecast correction 6fe39f7 (local equivalent c8db9e3). Raw results retain assembled-source SHA256.

Four normal seed42 histories compare small settlement and regional towns under public-first versus private-first policy over twelve months, plus an exact thirty-day replay check. Mayor actions purchase an actual square, pay four gatherings, review a staged grain order, and seek private teaching where permitted. Public-first budgets follow current operating standards; private-first authorizes vouchers and initially reduces public education/fire purchases. No funds, workforce, stock, closures or migrants are manufactured. Policies differ in multiple respects; this is a bundled playtest, not a school-only causal experiment.

All histories pass cash/goods/town validation and exact archive continuation. Providers enter through funded construction and actual qualified staffing. Private provision does not replace full public coverage automatically. In the regional public-first arm, one private school has no clients and only 5.49 cash at the endpoint: public competition can make a provider weak, rather than guarantee its profit. That provider is not forcibly rescued by this playtest.

No naturally closed industrial site becomes available in these short histories. The mayor does not close a healthy employer simply to unlock renewal. Therefore no ordinary renewal benefit is claimed. The separate existing brownfield suite passes on the same combined source: explicitly controlled quarry closure, recorded-owner compensation, non-refundable assessment, open-trade/access-dependent paid ground works and separately funded park/home/grocer reuse. Its synthetic closure is accounting evidence, not natural pacing evidence.

Gatherings cost 228 each and produce 38–48 modelled visits; total preparation per town is 912. They provide no economic return or score buff. They cannot be exploited for income, but are inexpensive optional decoration compared with service budgets. Calling that a compelling strategic tradeoff would be a design judgment unsupported by these automated runs. The more consequential choice is treasury accumulation versus health/education coverage. Both policy bundles remain comfortable over this horizon; no hard recovery dilemma emerges. This does not establish a dominant long-run strategy or prove the game is fun.

Most important finding: no new reproducible combined-system correctness defect. No production tuning or speculative fix added. Player-facing explanation: fund recurring services separately from one-time places and gatherings; private providers need paying demand, qualified workers and cash; staged-order receipts belong to suppliers; renewal first requires an actual closed site, compensation and ground works, then new construction capital. Larger treasury balances alone do not establish a healthier city.
