# Retail staffing and unmet food: focused diagnosis

The deterministic replay and measurements are in [`grocer-capacity-response.json`](grocer-capacity-response.json); run with `python tests/test_grocer_capacity_response.py`.

## Finding

On the regional seed 909 after a 120-day external road interruption and 120-day reopening, day 301 had 711 residents, 535 filled jobs, 72 open job slots, 13 groceries, and no eligible unassigned workers. The stores’ combined stocked-and-staffed daily sales capacity was 1,120 units against estimated household demand of 1,137.6; daily unmet food was 17.6 units. The prior 30 days recorded 2,627.2 unmet units. Household audit in the parent shortage report shows this is rotating late shoppers after accessible staffed capacity has been used, not townwide empty stock or a general affordability failure.

This was not evidence that a profitable grocery was refusing to expand. The roster includes ten grocers with one unfilled job each, three with zero staff, and fully staffed stores as well. Across all sectors, job demand exceeds available eligible residents: `assignJobs()` can fill vacancies only from already resident, age-eligible, unemployed people with a connected home/work route and allowed commute distance; it does not poach workers from another employer. Hiring decisions in `planEnterprise()` may raise desired jobs from realized sales, but vacancies are not filled without that worker pool. At day 301 there were 0 eligible free workers, despite 72 aggregate vacancies.

The city’s commercial-investment forecast also does not directly see the shortage. `cityDemand()` estimates grocer applicants from residents divided by the number of groceries and applies only a small five-point bonus when a shop’s inventory drops below 35. It ignores `G.market.unmetFood` and staffed/sellable capacity. Here the grocer applicant score was 16.15, overall business demand 77, and automatic development continued constructing commercial buildings; that score cannot establish expected grocery throughput or a labor supply.

A matched ordinary mayor-built grocer cost $4,050 plus $12,000 regional capital. After 30 days it had 893.8 food units, two open vacancies, zero workers, zero sales, and negative net profit; town sellable capacity remained 1,120. Incumbent worker identities were unchanged. This confirms that building a stocked store is not a remedy when no hireable residents exist. The ordinary home-build attempt at the sampled city location was not a valid labor remedy either: it added two residents and increased household need, while they were not employed at the grocer. A control replay was retained; natural 30-day unmet totals vary materially, so those totals are not interpreted as an intervention effect. Every arm passed town validation and cash/goods conservation (cash drift under $0.000003; goods error under 0.000000002 units).

## Implication and scope

The supported diagnosis is a labor/placement constraint with a development-forecast blind spot, not an isolated retailer expansion bug. A clean causal improvement would make commercial siting/forecasting account for reachable household catchments, observed food shortfalls, staffed capacity and realistic reachable labor; UI should distinguish “food need,” “on-hand inventory,” “sellable today,” and “available workers.” More labor supply must come through credible population and housing/migration or effective transit changes, not forced job transfers, fabricated households or higher per-worker output. These are proposals, not changes made here.

This is one city seed and one interruption/reopening history, with a 30-day action comparison. It does not establish a general shortage rate or prove which policy will recover service. No production coefficients or economy rules were changed.
